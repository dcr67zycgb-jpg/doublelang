Russian Verb Aspect: How to Stop Confusing Делать and Сделать

🕐 12 min read🇷🇺 Россия📅 Актуально на 2026 год

If you've been learning Russian for more than a week, you've probably noticed something peculiar: almost every verb seems to have a twin. Why does Russian need both делать (delat') and сделать (sdelat') when English gets by with just "to do"? The answer lies in Russian verb aspect, one of the most distinctive features of Slavic grammar.

Understanding Russian verb aspect isn't just about memorizing pairs. It's about learning to see actions differently, the way native speakers do. One verb tells you what happened, the other tells you how it happened, whether it finished, and what the speaker wants you to focus on. Once this clicks, you'll stop translating word-for-word and start thinking in Russian.

In this guide, we'll break down the logic behind perfective and imperfective verbs, show you how to recognize and form aspectual pairs, and give you practical decision-making rules you can use in real conversations.

What Exactly Is Russian Verb Aspect?

Russian verb aspect is a grammatical category that shows how an action unfolds in time. Every Russian verb belongs to one of two aspects: imperfective (несовершенный вид, nesovershenny vid) or perfective (совершенный вид, sovershennyy vid). Most verbs come in aspectual pairs that share the same basic meaning but differ in how they present the action.

The imperfective aspect focuses on the process, repetition, or ongoing nature of an action. It doesn't tell you whether the action was completed. When you say Я читал книгу (Ya chital knigu, "I was reading a book"), you're emphasizing the activity itself, not whether you finished.

The perfective aspect, on the other hand, presents an action as a complete, singular event with a clear result. Я прочитал книгу (Ya prochital knigu, "I read the book") tells your listener that you finished it, cover to cover.

Key insight: Aspect isn't about time (past, present, future), it's about how you frame the action. You can talk about the past using either aspect, depending on what you want to emphasize.

Here's something that trips up learners: perfective verbs have no present tense. When you conjugate a perfective verb in what looks like the present tense (я сделаю, ya sdelayu), you're actually forming the future. Imperfective verbs, meanwhile, have all three tenses, including a compound future (я буду делать, ya budu delat', "I will be doing").

How Russian Verb Aspect Rules Actually Work

The Completion Test

The single most reliable way to choose between aspects is to ask: does the sentence emphasize that the action was (or will be) completed with a result? If yes, use perfective. If the focus is on the process, duration, or the fact that the action happened without stressing completion, use imperfective.

Compare these sentences:

  • Вчера я писал письмо (Vchera ya pisal pis'mo) - Yesterday I was writing a letter (imperfective: you were engaged in writing, may or may not have finished)
  • Вчера я написал письмо (Vchera ya napisal pis'mo) - Yesterday I wrote a letter (perfective: the letter is done, probably sent)

Both are grammatically correct. Your choice depends entirely on what you want to communicate. This isn't arbitrary, it carries real meaning that native speakers pick up instantly.

Repetition and Habit

Whenever an action repeats, happens regularly, or is habitual, you must use the imperfective aspect. Perfective verbs describe single, complete events, so they can't express repetition on their own.

Каждое утро я читаю газету (Kazhdoe utro ya chitayu gazetu, "Every morning I read the newspaper") uses the imperfective читаю because it's a repeated action. You can't say *каждое утро я прочитаю, it makes no sense to a native speaker.

Time expressions like часто (chasto, often), всегда (vsegda, always), иногда (inogda, sometimes), каждый день (kazhdyy den', every day) are strong signals to use imperfective verbs.

Process vs Result in Context

Sometimes the same action can be framed either way, and your aspect choice changes the implication. Consider asking someone what they did yesterday:

Что ты делал вчера? (Chto ty delal vchera?) - What were you doing yesterday? (interested in how you spent your time)

Что ты сделал вчера? (Chto ty sdelal vchera?) - What did you accomplish yesterday? (interested in results, what you completed)

The first question is casual, open-ended. The second carries an expectation of achievement. Parents asking their teenager about homework would likely use the perfective, your friend asking about your day off would use imperfective.

Russian Verb Aspect Rules for Beginners: Start Here

If you're just starting out, these three rules will cover about 80% of situations:

Use imperfective for ongoing or repeated actions

Anytime you're describing a process in progress, something habitual, or an action that happened multiple times, reach for the imperfective. Я учил русский язык пять лет (Ya uchil russkiy yazyk pyat' let, "I studied Russian for five years") - the focus is on the duration and process.

Use perfective for completed actions with results

When the action is finished and produced a concrete outcome, use perfective. Я выучил это слово (Ya vyuchil eto slovo, "I learned this word") - now you know it, the learning is complete.

Use perfective for single future actions

When talking about something you'll do once in the future as a complete action, perfective is your default. Завтра я куплю хлеб (Zavtra ya kuplyu khleb, "Tomorrow I'll buy bread") - a single, complete purchase.

Check for time markers

Words like уже (uzhe, already) and наконец (nakonets, finally) signal completed actions, so use perfective. Words like долго (dolgo, for a long time) and всё утро (vsyo utro, all morning) emphasize duration, so use imperfective.

Common Aspectual Pairs: Perfective and Imperfective Verbs Russian

Most Russian verbs exist in aspectual pairs. Learning these pairs is non-negotiable if you want to speak naturally. The perfective partner is usually formed by adding a prefix or changing the suffix, though some pairs are completely different words.

Prefixation Patterns

The most common way to form perfective verbs is by adding a prefix to an imperfective base. The prefix с-/со- appears frequently:

ImperfectivePerfectiveMeaning
делать (delat')сделать (sdelat')to do, to make
писать (pisat')написать (napisat')to write
читать (chitat')прочитать (prochitat')to read
готовить (gotovit')приготовить (prigotovit')to cook, to prepare
говорить (govorit')сказать (skazat')to speak, to say
покупать (pokupat')купить (kupit')to buy

Notice that говорить/сказать is an irregular pair, the perfective isn't just prefixed but uses a different root. These suppletive pairs need to be memorized separately.

Suffix Changes

Some verbs form their aspectual partners through suffix alternation, typically -ать/-ить in the imperfective becoming -ить/-ать or other changes in the perfective:

ImperfectivePerfectiveMeaning
решать (reshat')решить (reshit')to solve, to decide
получать (poluchat')получить (poluchit')to receive, to get
встречать (vstrechat')встретить (vstretit')to meet
отвечать (otvechat')ответить (otvetit')to answer

Watch out: Adding a prefix sometimes creates a completely different verb, not just a perfective partner. Писать (to write) becomes написать (to write, perfective), but описать means "to describe" and переписать means "to rewrite." Context and dictionaries are your friends here.

Imperfective Verbs Russian List You'll Use Daily

Here are high-frequency imperfective verbs you'll encounter constantly, along with their perfective pairs:

  • знать (znat', to know) - no perfective pair, it's a state verb
  • видеть/увидеть (videt'/uvidet', to see)
  • слышать/услышать (slyshat'/uslyshat', to hear)
  • понимать/понять (ponimat'/ponyat', to understand)
  • учить/выучить (uchit'/vyuchit', to learn, to memorize)
  • учиться/научиться (uchit'sya/nauchit'sya, to study, to learn how)
  • помогать/помочь (pomogat'/pomoch', to help)
  • начинать/начать (nachinat'/nachat', to begin)
  • заканчивать/закончить (zakanchivat'/zakonchit', to finish)

State verbs like знать, любить (lyubit', to love), and хотеть (khotet', to want) typically exist only in the imperfective because they describe ongoing states, not actions that can be completed.

How to Choose Verb Aspect in Russian Sentence Construction

Negation Changes Everything

Here's a curveball: when you negate an action in the past tense, you almost always use the imperfective, even if you're talking about a single action that didn't reach completion.

Я не читал эту книгу (Ya ne chital etu knigu, "I haven't read this book") uses imperfective because you're denying the action ever took place. Using perfective *не прочитал would mean you started but didn't finish, a different meaning entirely.

But in the future tense, negation with perfective is common when you're stating you won't do something as a decision: Я не куплю это (Ya ne kuplyu eto, "I won't buy this").

Imperatives and Invitations

Commands and requests follow their own logic. For polite invitations or offers, use imperfective: Садитесь, пожалуйста (Sadites', pozhaluysta, "Please, sit down"). For specific instructions or requests for completion, use perfective: Закройте дверь (Zakroyte dver', "Close the door").

Negative imperatives (telling someone not to do something) usually take imperfective: Не говори так (Ne govori tak, "Don't talk like that"). Using perfective in negative commands often sounds like a warning against accidentally doing something: Не упади! (Ne upadi!, "Don't fall!").

Sequential Actions

When you're narrating a series of completed actions, use perfective verbs to move the story forward: Я встал, оделся, позавтракал и вышел (Ya vstal, odelsya, pozavtrakal i vyshel, "I got up, got dressed, had breakfast, and left"). Each action is complete before the next begins.

If you want to describe simultaneous ongoing actions or background context, switch to imperfective: Пока я готовил ужин, дети играли (Poka ya gotovil uzhin, deti igrali, "While I was cooking dinner, the children were playing").

Delat vs Sdelat: The Classic Example Unpacked

The pair делать/сделать (delat'/sdelat') perfectly illustrates how Russian verb aspect works in practice. Both mean "to do" or "to make," but they're not interchangeable.

Delat vs Sdelat Examples in Sentences

Что ты делаешь? (Chto ty delaesh'?) - What are you doing? (right now, process)

Что ты сделаешь завтра? (Chto ty sdelaesh' zavtra?) - What will you do tomorrow? (what specific tasks will you complete)

In the past tense:

Я делал домашнюю работу весь вечер (Ya delal domashnyuyu rabotu ves' vecher) - I was doing homework all evening (emphasis on duration, you spent the evening on it)

Я сделал домашнюю работу за час (Ya sdelal domashnyuyu rabotu za chas) - I did my homework in an hour (emphasis on completion and efficiency)

Notice how the perfective version pairs naturally with за час (za chas, "in an hour"), while the imperfective works with весь вечер (ves' vecher, "all evening"). These time expressions reinforce the aspectual meaning.

The Difference Between Delat and Sdelat in Questions

When asking about completed actions or requesting something be done, use perfective: Ты сделал уроки? (Ty sdelal uroki?, "Did you do your homework?", implying "is it done?").

When asking about activities or how someone spent time, use imperfective: Что ты делал на выходных? (Chto ty delal na vykhodnykh?, "What did you do on the weekend?", asking about activities generally).

This distinction exists across all aspectual pairs, not just делать/сделать. Getting it right makes you sound dramatically more natural and helps you understand the nuances native speakers pack into their aspect choices.

Russian Verb Aspect and Tenses: Past, Present, Future

Past Tense Russian Aspect Choices

The past tense is where aspect choice matters most and causes the most confusion. Both aspects use the same past tense formation (the -л suffix), but they tell different stories.

Use imperfective past when:

  • The action was in progress at a specific moment: В три часа я обедал (V tri chasa ya obedal, "At three o'clock I was having lunch")
  • The action repeated: Каждый день мы гуляли (Kazhdyy den' my gulyali, "Every day we went for walks")
  • You're describing how time was spent without focusing on results: Вчера мы смотрели фильмы (Vchera my smotreli fil'my, "Yesterday we watched movies")

Use perfective past when:

  • The action completed with a result: Я починил машину (Ya pochinil mashinu, "I fixed the car", it now works)
  • You're narrating a sequence: Он пришёл, сел и начал работать (On prishyol, sel i nachal rabotat', "He came, sat down, and started working")
  • The completion itself is newsworthy: Наконец-то я сдал экзамен! (Nakonets-to ya sdal ekzamen!, "I finally passed the exam!")

Future Tense Russian Verbs

Future tense has two completely different formations depending on aspect. Perfective verbs form a simple future that looks exactly like present tense conjugation: я сделаю, ты сделаешь, он сделает (ya sdelayu, ty sdelaesh', on sdelaet).

Imperfective verbs form a compound future with быть (byt', to be) plus the infinitive: я буду делать, ты будешь делать, он будет делать (ya budu delat', ty budesh' delat', on budet delat').

This grammatical difference reinforces the semantic one: perfective future presents a future action as a single complete event, imperfective future presents it as an ongoing process or repeated action.

If you're talking about your plans as definite completed actions, use perfective: Завтра я встречусь с другом (Zavtra ya vstrechius' s drugom, "Tomorrow I'll meet with a friend", one meeting, will happen). If you're describing future activities or processes, use imperfective: Летом я буду много читать (Letom ya budu mnogo chitat', "In summer I'll read a lot", ongoing activity).

Practical Exercises: Russian Verb Aspect in Action

Theory only gets you so far. You need to practice making aspect decisions in context until it becomes automatic. Here are scenarios that will test your understanding.

Russian Verb Aspect Exercises With Answers

Try these sentences. Should you use imperfective or perfective?

1. Yesterday I _____ (read) for three hours. [читал - imperfective, emphasizes duration]

2. Yesterday I _____ (read) the entire book. [прочитал - perfective, emphasizes completion]

3. Every morning she _____ (drink) coffee. [пьёт - imperfective, habitual action]

4. She _____ (drink) her coffee and left. [выпила - perfective, completed action in sequence]

5. I _____ (not see) this film. [не видел - imperfective, denying the action occurred]

6. When you _____ (see) him, tell him to call me. [увидишь - perfective, single future completed action]

7. We _____ (discuss) this problem for two hours. [обсуждали - imperfective, process with duration]

8. We _____ (discuss) this problem and found a solution. [обсудили - perfective, completed with result]

If you got most of these right, you're developing the right instincts. If not, don't worry, this is genuinely difficult and takes time. The key is to stop translating and start thinking about whether you want to emphasize process, repetition, and duration (imperfective) or completion, result, and single events (perfective).

Using Aspect With Verbs of Motion

Aspect interacts with another uniquely Russian category: verbs of motion. Motion verbs already come in determinate/indeterminate pairs (like идти/ходить), and then each of those can take perfective prefixes.

The base pairs are imperfective: идти (idti, to go on foot, one direction) and ходить (khodit', to go habitually or multi-directionally). Add prefixes and you get perfective motion verbs: прийти (priyti, to arrive), уйти (uyti, to leave), войти (voyti, to enter).

This creates a three-way system that's honestly complex enough for its own article, but the aspect logic remains the same: perfective motion verbs describe single completed trips, imperfective ones describe processes, repetition, or general ability to go somewhere.

Why Learning Russian Verb Aspect Matters for Exams and Real Life

If you're preparing for TORFL or another Russian proficiency exam, aspect errors will cost you points across all sections: writing, speaking, and even reading comprehension, where recognizing aspect helps you understand nuance.

TORFL graders specifically watch for appropriate aspect use in narratives (perfective for event sequences), descriptions (imperfective for background and states), and dialogues (correct aspect in questions and imperatives). Getting aspect right is one of the clearest markers that separates intermediate from advanced learners.

Beyond exams, aspect mistakes change meaning in ways that confuse native speakers. Saying *Я читал эту книгу when you meant to say you finished it leaves your listener wondering whether you actually completed it. Using perfective when describing a process makes you sound abrupt or impatient.

The good news? Once you internalize aspect, your Russian doesn't just become more grammatically correct, it becomes more expressive. You can convey subtle differences in meaning that English requires whole phrases to express. You'll understand why Russians can have two different verbs for "to give" (давать/дать) and why both are necessary.

Moving Forward: From Theory to Intuition

Aspect won't click overnight, and that's fine. Native speakers acquire it gradually as children, and you're doing it as an adult while also learning vocabulary, cases, and everything else Russian throws at you.

Start by focusing on the most common pairs: делать/сделать, читать/прочитать, писать/написать, говорить/сказать. Get comfortable with these in different tenses and contexts before expanding to others. When you read Russian or listen to native speakers, pay attention to which aspect they choose and try to figure out why.

Work with actual texts and conversations, not just isolated sentences. Aspect choice often depends on surrounding context, what came before, what the speaker wants to emphasize, what the listener already knows. Exercises help, but immersion in real Russian is where intuition develops.

If you're working with a tutor (and you should be, aspect is one of those topics where personalized feedback accelerates progress enormously), ask them to correct your aspect errors specifically and explain the reasoning. Many learners get corrections on cases and word order but let aspect mistakes slide because the sentence is technically understandable. Don't let that happen, insist on precision here.

Russian verb aspect is learnable. Thousands of non-native speakers have mastered it, and you can too. It requires patience, attention, and lots of practice, but the payoff is enormous: you'll finally sound like you know what you're talking about, and you'll understand Russian with a depth that pure vocabulary memorization can never give you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between perfective and imperfective verbs in Russian?

Imperfective verbs describe actions as processes, ongoing states, or repeated events without indicating completion. Perfective verbs present actions as completed events with results. For example, читать (chitat', imperfective) means "to read" or "to be reading," while прочитать (prochitat', perfective) means "to finish reading" or "to have read." The choice depends on whether you want to emphasize the action itself or its completion.

How to know if a Russian verb is perfective or imperfective?

Dictionaries always mark aspect (несов./impf. for imperfective, сов./pf. for perfective). Perfective verbs often have prefixes like с-, на-, про-, вы- added to imperfective bases, though this isn't universal. Grammatically, if a verb has a present tense form that describes current action, it's imperfective; perfective verbs have no true present tense, only past and future. With practice, you'll recognize common patterns and pairs.

When to use perfective vs imperfective in Russian?

Use imperfective for ongoing processes, habitual actions, repeated events, or when emphasizing duration without focusing on completion. Use perfective for single completed actions with results, sequential events in narratives, or when the fact of completion is important. In questions, imperfective asks about activities or processes (что ты делал?, "what were you doing?"), while perfective asks about results (что ты сделал?, "what did you get done?").

Why does Russian have two verb aspects?

Aspect allows Russian speakers to express nuances about how actions unfold in time without extra words. Where English uses adverbs, helping verbs, or context ("I was reading," "I read through," "I used to read"), Russian builds this directly into verb choice. It's a fundamental feature of Slavic languages that provides precision in describing whether actions are completed, ongoing, repeated, or simply occurred, enriching the language's expressive capacity.

How to form perfective verbs in Russian?

Most perfective verbs are formed by adding prefixes to imperfective bases: делать → сделать, читать → прочитать. Some use suffix changes: решать → решить, получать → получить. A few pairs are suppletive, using different roots entirely: говорить → сказать, брать → взять. There's no single rule; you learn aspectual pairs as vocabulary. Good dictionaries and textbooks list both partners together.

What is the difference between delat and sdelat?

Делать (delat') is imperfective and means "to do" or "to be doing," emphasizing the process or habitual action: Я делаю уроки ("I'm doing homework"). Сделать (sdelat') is perfective and means "to complete doing," emphasizing the result: Я сделал уроки ("I finished my homework"). The imperfective can describe ongoing or repeated action; the perfective describes a single completed act. Context and what you want to emphasize determines which to use.
